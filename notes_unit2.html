Unit 2: Current Electricity (Long Answer Study Guide)

1. Microscopic Model of Current

Consider a conductor with cross-sectional area A and an electric field E applied from right to left. Suppose there are n electrons per unit volume (number density) moving with a drift velocity vd.

[Insert Figure: Microscopic model of current showing a section of conductor with length dx and area A]
  • The electrons move a distance dx = vd dt in a small time interval dt.
  • The number of electrons in the volume element (V = A dx) is n A dx.
  • Substituting dx: Total number of electrons = n A vd dt.
  • Total charge in this volume: dQ = (number of electrons) × (charge of an electron) = (n A vd dt)e.
  • Current is defined as the rate of flow of charge: I = dQ / dt.
I = n e A vd

2. Microscopic Form of Ohm's Law

Current density J is the current per unit area of cross-section: J = I / A. From the above derivation, J = n e vd.

Substituting the expression for drift velocity: vd = (eτ / m) E

  • J = n e (eτ / m) E = (n e² τ / m) E
  • The quantity (n e² τ / m) is a constant for a given material at a given temperature, called Conductivity (σ).
J = σE

In vector form, J = σE. This is the microscopic form of Ohm's Law.

3. Macroscopic Form of Ohm's Law

Consider a segment of wire of length l and cross-sectional area A. If a potential difference V is applied, the electric field is E = V / l.

Using the microscopic law J = σE:

  • Substitute J = I / A and E = V / l: I / A = σ (V / l)
  • Rearranging for V: V = I (l / σA)
  • The term (l / σA) is the resistance R of the conductor.
V = IR

Limitations: Ohm's law is not a fundamental law. It holds only for "Ohmic" materials where resistance is constant. It fails for non-ohmic devices like vacuum diodes, transistors, and semiconductors where the V-I relationship is non-linear.

4. Internal Resistance of a Cell using Voltmeter

The EMF (ε) of a cell is the voltage when no current is drawn. Due to the internal resistance r, the potential difference V across the cell in a closed circuit is less than ε.

[Insert Figure: Circuit showing a cell with internal resistance r, an external resistor R, and a voltmeter]
[Image of internal resistance of a cell circuit diagram]
  • In an open circuit, the voltmeter reads ε.
  • When resistance R is connected, current I = ε / (R + r).
  • The potential difference across R is V = IR.
  • Substituting I: V = εR / (R + r).
  • Rearranging: V(R + r) = εR ⇒ VR + Vr = εR ⇒ Vr = εR - VR.
r = R [ (ε - V) / V ]

5. Kirchhoff’s Rules

I. Junction Rule (Current Rule): The algebraic sum of currents meeting at any junction in a circuit is zero (ΣI = 0). Currents entering are positive; currents leaving are negative. This is based on Conservation of Charge.

II. Loop Rule (Voltage Rule): In any closed part of a circuit, the algebraic sum of the products of current and resistance (IR) is equal to the algebraic sum of the EMFs in that loop (ΣIR = Σε). This is based on Conservation of Energy.

6. Wheatstone’s Bridge Balance Condition

Applying Kirchhoff's rules to a bridge with resistances P, Q, R, S and galvanometer G. Let IG be the galvanometer current.

[Insert Figure: Wheatstone’s bridge circuit diagram]
[Image of Wheatstone's bridge]
  • At Junction B: I1 - IG - I3 = 0
  • At Junction D: I2 + IG - I4 = 0
  • For Loop ABDA: I1P + IGG - I2R = 0
  • The bridge is balanced when IG = 0.
  • This implies I1 = I3 and I2 = I4.
  • The loop equation becomes I1P = I2R.
  • Applying loop rule to BCDB: I3Q - I4S - IGG = 0 ⇒ I1Q = I2S.
P / Q = R / S

7. Meter Bridge (Determination of Unknown Resistance)

The meter bridge consists of a 1m manganin wire. An unknown resistance P is in the left gap and a known Q is in the right gap.

[Insert Figure: Meter bridge circuit showing gaps, wire AB, and jockey J]
[Image of Meter bridge circuit]
  • The jockey is moved to find a null point J where the galvanometer shows zero.
  • Let AJ = l1 and JB = l2.
  • By Wheatstone’s principle: P / Q = (Resistance of AJ) / (Resistance of JB).
  • If r' is resistance per unit length: P / Q = (r' l1) / (r' l2).
P = Q (l1 / l2)

8. Comparison of EMF using Potentiometer

The principle of a potentiometer is that the potential drop is proportional to the length of the wire (V ∝ l).

[Insert Figure: Potentiometer circuit with primary circuit and secondary circuit containing two cells]
  • A constant current I is maintained in the primary circuit.
  • For cell 1 (ε1), the balancing length is l1: ε1 = I r' l1.
  • For cell 2 (ε2), the balancing length is l2: ε2 = I r' l2.
  • By dividing the two equations:
ε1 / ε2 = l1 / l2
